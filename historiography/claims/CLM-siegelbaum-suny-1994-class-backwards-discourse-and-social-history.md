## 核心论断命题（Thesis）
刘易斯·西格尔鲍姆（Lewis H. Siegelbaum）与罗纳德·苏尼（Ronald Grigor Suny）在《阶级倒退了吗？探寻苏联工人阶级》导论中，既拒斥汤普森式“文化主义/经验主义”将阶级归结为原生经验的本质论，也反对斯特德曼·琼斯等“纯语言学转向”将社会世界消融为文本话语的做法，主张将**生产关系与再生产约束**同**话语建构及权力角逐（政治）**结合起来分析；由此追踪俄国无产阶级从“1905–1917 年形成→内战去阶级化与国家替代解体→1920 年代新经济政策下作为‘政权忠诚政治建构’的重塑”三段悖论历程，指出苏联工人在非资本主义环境中完成了人口意义上的无产阶级化，却因失去独立政治组织与表达空间而未能构成马克思意义上的“自为阶级”。

## 论证链条与依据（Reasoning & Evidence）
1. **超越“汤普森经验主义 vs. 语言学转向”的理论综合**（`orig. pp. 4–8` / `PDF pp. 21–25`）：
   - 梳理 E. P. Thompson（1963）以“文化/经验”取代结构决定论、却将“自在阶级”与“自为阶级”混为一谈并遭到阿尔都塞派与佩里·安德森批判的学术史（`pp. 4–5`）；进而检讨 Gareth Stedman Jones（1983）、William H. Sewell Jr.（1980）与 Joan W. Scott（1988）“语言/性别先行于并界定利益”的后结构主义冲击（`pp. 5–8`）。
   - 两位编者提出中间综合命题：语言既非单纯反映“社会存在”，亦非先验单方面制造社会现实；社会分工中的物质约束、个体/群体身份认同与阶级话语三者相互作用，而围绕意义与忠诚的争夺即是“政治”（`p. 8`）。
2. **第一阶段（帝俄晚期—1917）的偶发性形成而非目的论展开**（`orig. pp. 8–13` / `PDF pp. 25–30`）：
   - 吸纳 Zelnik（小组 *kruzhok* 与传记经验）、Steinberg（人性尊严道德话语）、Hogan（1905–1912 车间泰勒化与选民团）与 S. A. Smith 的研究，同时引入 Laura Engelstein 的性别排除警示，指出 1917 年革命激进性是政治斗争与多重排斥边界的偶发产物，而非先验“历史使命”的发现。
3. **第二阶段（1918–1921）内战解体与“无产阶级专政无产者”替代机制**（`orig. pp. 13–17` / `PDF pp. 30–34`）：
   - 工业工人从 1917 年约 300 万骤降至 1920 年底刚逾 100 万，引述 Isaac Deutscher“无产阶级专政获胜而无产阶级几近消失”、John Keep“乡村化”与 Sheila Fitzpatrick“柴郡猫的微笑”（`p. 14`）；党委员会取代工厂委员会与苏维埃，把抗议或求生工人（如顿巴斯矿工、印刷工）打成“失去阶级属性/小资产阶级”（`pp. 14–16`）。
   - 同时纳入 William Chase“分裂阶级（bifurcated class）”与 Diane Koenker 1985 年关于“阶级意识从车间向邻里、家庭与世俗城市文化迁移”的修正，反对简单化“去无产阶级化”比喻（`p. 15`）。
4. **第三阶段（1920 年代 NEP—1930 年代）无产阶级化却丧失“自为”政治空间**（`orig. pp. 17–26` / `PDF pp. 34–43`）：
   - 指出 1920 年代苏联呈现核心悖论：工业劳动力人口恢复，但作为“赋能能指（signifier of empowerment）”的工人阶级却失去锋芒，反而转化为**党国多义词库中的政治建构——使用阶级语言标志着对政权及其目标的忠诚**（`pp. 17–20`），如“大转变”后在“流沙社会（quicksand society）”中被彻底纳入“讲布尔什维克语”的单一国家剧本，工人虽有自身认同，却彻底失去国家规定之外的自主组织与议程空间（`pp. 20–26`）。

## 原文直引（Verbatim Quote）
> "Yet, workers still occupy positions within the social division of labor, and productive and reproductive relations continue to have a constraining power in people's lives. The point is not that language prefigures these relations any more than it merely reflects them. It is that social relations, individual and collective identities, and their representation in language work on each other in complex ways to generate particular understandings of class and collective behavior or action. Neither the social nor the discursive determine behavior by themselves in unmediated ways. They coexist and cannot be easily disentangled." `[orig. p. 8 | PDF p. 25 | ch05_class_backwards_in_search_of_the_soviet.md#L344-L353]`

> "As Isaac Deutscher put it, 'The proletarian dictatorship was triumphant, but the proletariat had nearly vanished.' ... The self-proclaimed vanguard of the proletariat, finding itself without a proletariat to lead, proceeded to rule alone, increasingly relying on its own apparatus as a substitute for the working class. 'Proletarian democracy,' one of the Bolsheviks' watchwords of 1917, was jettisoned in favor of the hierarchical rule of Communist Party committees, to which the factory committees, the soviets, and other authentic working-class organizations were subordinated." `[orig. p. 14 | PDF p. 31 | ch05_class_backwards_in_search_of_the_soviet.md#L624-L637]`

> "Our search for a Soviet working class has come up against a paradox. In the course of the 1920s, the industrial workforce was restored: yet the working class as a signifier of empowerment lost its salience. Small-scale entrepreneurs, NEPmen, and specialists could all arouse workers' ire—or envy. But neither the otherness of these groups, nor the party's totalizing rhetoric of the working class in power, nor even the commonality of wage labor made class imminent as a set of common understandings or a basis for collective action. Rather than assume that class was something that was fragmenting or pulling apart, we might do better to conceive of it as an emerging political construct whose employment signified loyalty to the regime and its goals." `[orig. pp. 19–20 | PDF pp. 36–37 | ch05_class_backwards_in_search_of_the_soviet.md#L888-L898]`

> "Soviet workers, coming together and working within a system called by its leaders 'socialist,' were quickly proletarianized, but they did not then constitute themselves as a conscious proletariat in Marx's sense of a class-for-itself. As Lewin points out, the working class had no 'other' in the form of an antagonistic bourgeoisie. It operated in a noncapitalist environment without the possibility of self-organization outside of state prescription. ... A Soviet industrial workforce existed and grew in the first two-and-a-half decades of Soviet power; its members certainly saw themselves as members of a 'working class'; but they had lost the political spaces in which they could develop independent conceptions of themselves and create their own agendas." `[orig. p. 26 | PDF p. 43 | ch05_class_backwards_in_search_of_the_soviet.md#L1208-L1223]`
