# Review of Anne O'Donnell, Power and Possession in the Russian Revolution

**Anne O'Donnell.**
*Power and Possession in the Russian Revolution.*
Princeton: Princeton University Press (http://press.princeton.edu/), 2024. xix + 370 pp.
$39.95 (cloth), ISBN 978-0-691-20554-0.

**Reviewed by** Peter Fraunholtz (Northeastern University)
**Published on** H-Russia (November, 2024)
**Commissioned by** Eva M. Stolberg (University of Duisburg-Essen, Germany)

*Power and Possession in the Russian Revolution,* Professor Anne O’Donnell’s exploration of the lived experience of dispossession and, in some cases, repossession beginning in 1917 reveals a complex array of engagements with the revolutionary process and its evolution. The focus here is on how lives and living spaces were torn apart and the sometimes convoluted revolutionary logic used by bureaucrats to makes sense of the process and eventually to stabilize an urban society that had been torn asunder. Indeed, the effort to transform Russia’s property relations was caught up in a whirlwind that resulted in chaos, fear, and suffering for the populations of major cities. The author does a quite admirable job of disentangling this complex combination of aspirational policies, multifaceted upheaval, and competition for authority and resources.

O’Donnell endeavors to tell the story of the unprecedented quest to abolish private property as the Soviet regime set about ridding Revolutionary Russia of all vestiges of capitalism in the pursuit of the transition to socialism. The Bolsheviks, in O’Donnell’s telling, sought to master Russia’s vast material wealth, an accurate accounting of which would reveal a material abundance that could serve as a solid foundation upon which to build a socialist system. *Power and Possession* concentrates on the dynamics of property dispossession in the metropoles, a process that parallels the overturning of property relations that transformed rural Russian. To that end, O’Donnell delves into the distinction between requisition (with compensation) and confiscation (without compensation) that was at times somewhat clearer on paper than in practice in Petrograd and Moscow. She provides a fascinating parallel here to debates between Kadets and SRs around these very terms as they pertained to landed property in 1917.

O’Donnell explores the fate of private property and its owners in two distinct stages. During 1917-1920, dispossession at the hand of various state entities “came to appear as a natural … element of the transition to socialism” as the legal, cultural, and political foundations of private property were dismantled. Her concern is how the “extraordinary unraveling happened.” For the most part, the process took the regime by surprise and transpired “largely in the absence of specific, central authorizing laws” (p. 4). The author conveys a clear and detailed narrative of how revolutionary dreamers let loose a wave of dispossession in the service of making the radical leap to a new kind of society. What they lacked was the capacity to control or even guide the process. Indeed, what was motivated, at best, by a drive for social justice created a wide arena for opportunism and criminality.

Dispossessed petitioners engaged with all manner of organizations and authorities as they lodged their petitions for reconsideration and redress, appealed unfavorable decisions, and persevered, occasionally successfully, in challenging the state’s claim on their possessions. Indeed, as O’Donnell makes plain, it is precisely because of the complaints and petitions that there are records available to document the extraordinary unraveling. To some extent, the vast scale of the appeals process reflected ordinary people’s engagement with the state inadvertently contributing to legitimizing the regime’s authority.

From spring 1920 through 1922, the Soviet state sought to know and document the material world and determine how best to assess the value of the various kinds of property in its possession, in the absences of markets. Once the end of the civil war was in sight the regime struggled to balance revolutionary transformation with a return to stability through rebuilding “bonds of possession” (p. 4). By mid-1920, the transformation of property relations sat squarely at the crossroads of revolutionary upheaval and social exhaustion. Moreover, by 1920 the Soviet leadership’s hope for revolution in capitalist states (and thus material assistance for building socialism) was fading quickly, resulting in the state’s urgency to fully assess its accumulated “valuables” for sale abroad in order to finance the purchase of imported necessities.

O’Donnell draws on a rich source base that includes popular petitions, apartment inspections, internal audits of revolutionary institutions, and records of the political police to illustrate how ordinary people and the personnel of the revolutionary state in its early decentralized form navigated the complex process of changing property relations, mostly after the 1917 Revolutions. Thanks to this source base, we encounter institutions fashioned by the revolution: Goskon (People’s Commissariat of Government Control, later Rabkrin), Gokhran (State Depository of Valuables, department of Narkomfin), ZhZ0 (Housing and Land Department of local soviets), and the Central Bureau of Complaints. The fate of private property involved the emergence and evolution of state bureaucracy and bureaucratic rivalry born of the practical challenges of the revolutionary mission. The author expertly details the post-1917 efforts of bureaucrats in multiple state institutions to balance the tensions between both competing theories and competing political demands in formulating the language of official decrees.

Each chapter opens with a vivid example of dispossession, investigation, or criminal proceeding that serves as an entree into critical features of the transformation and the stages through which it passed. During dispossession there are often multiple rival institutions staking claims to property and the authority to act in the name of the people. Post dispossession, petitioners engaged with all manner of organizations and authorities as they lodged their appeals for reconsideration and redress. By 1920 as the state sought to create some legal foundation to account for what had transpired and some degree of return to normalcy, stakeholder institutions including rival commissariats, Narkomfin and Narkomiust for example, weighed in with competing views on legal language and corrective action. *Power and Possession* illustrates how the Bolsheviks’ path from determined ideologues atop a fragmented state apparatus to somewhat effective centralized power was marked by a collision between transformational state aspirations and a multiplicity of bureaucratic and popular responses.

It makes a certain amount of sense to start the story of the dismantling of private property with the regime seeking to implement a maximalist agenda. Yet to the extent that popular forces in 1917 embraced the Bolshevik assault on private property, recent scholarship on the lived experience of the home front during the war years of 1914–17 could provide some valuable context for the unraveling to come. O’Donnell does note official decrees on alien-owned property that provoked confiscation of rural estates. Yet accelerating inflation spurred attacks on the property of presumed speculators, and in a variety of lower-class spheres a sense of the uneven burdens of war mobilization contributed to a social environment increasingly divided along class lines and infused with a growing sense of entitlement among those who had sacrificed for the war effort. Bolshevik decrees on private property found an audience eager to participate in their implementation.

O’Donnell’s narrative of the fate of private property and the evolution of the state’s management of the process does well to highlight the key turning point in 1920 toward stabilization amidst civil war victory and widespread social exhaustion after years of upheaval. Less clear is the relationship between the early stages of dispossession and the threats posed to the Bolshevik regime teetering on the brink of collapse from March to September 1918. The September 13, 1918, “Order on the Defense of the city of Moscow … from parasites” (p. 90) published in *Izvestiia* VTsIK motivated local organs to begin a wave of evictions just days after the attempt on Lenin’s life and at the height of the urban food crisis.

Rightfully so, O’Donnell skillfully illustrates the blurring of lines between official practices of dispossession and the opportunities for criminality that emerged in the guise of taking action on behalf of the people. Indeed, the author suggests some line-blurring between corruption and pragmatism involved in “out of turn” distribution (p. 176) motivated, in part, by the need to clear overcrowded goods-storage space but also for compensating the efforts and loyalty of skilled personnel amidst the Bolsheviks’ desperate efforts to solidify their power. In the absence of market forces, state-provided security of living space and possessions no doubt became essential currency when monied salaries had become useless.

*Power and Possession* offers a clear picture of how the Soviet state sought to know and document the urban material world. It provides a welcome addition to the growing literature on the effort of reformist elements within the bureaucracy that spanned the three regimes seeking to develop appropriate norms of peasant land holding, more effective means of evaluating household productivity for the purposes of tax assessment, and consumption norms used as a benchmark for clarifying how much surplus grain the state monopoly could lay claim to. The rationalizing impulse, accelerated during the war, that contributed to norming many features of Russian life was predicated on the state developing the means of discovering, in ample detail, its own population.

O’Donnell’s investigation of the fate of private property highlights the extent to which 1917-22 was a period of constant tension between ideology and practical political power, struggle for authority between state institutions and organs of local self-government, and dynamic engagement between a fragmented state apparatus and ordinary people seeking to defend their interests amidst radical social and political change. It is a welcome addition to the literature on the 1917 Revolutions and the Bolsheviks’ struggles to transform Russia.
